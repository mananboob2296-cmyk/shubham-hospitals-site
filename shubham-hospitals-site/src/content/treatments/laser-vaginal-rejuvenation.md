## What it is

Laser vaginal rejuvenation is a non-surgical, clinic-based treatment in which a small laser probe delivers controlled pulses of energy to the lining of the vagina, and sometimes to the vulva. The two lasers most often used are the fractional CO2 laser and the erbium (Er:YAG) laser. The idea is that tiny, controlled areas of heating prompt the tissue to repair itself, laying down new collagen and improving moisture and elasticity over the following weeks.

You may see it advertised as "laser vaginal tightening", "vaginal laser therapy" or "intimate laser". These describe broadly the same treatment. It is one of the options offered by our [cosmetic and aesthetic gynaecology unit](/treatments/cosmetic-aesthetic-gynecology/), and you can [see all the procedures we perform](/procedures/).

I want to be clear from the start about what laser can and cannot do, because the marketing around it often is not. It is not surgery, it does not change the size or shape of the labia, it does not repair a prolapse or a torn perineum, and it does not reverse age. In carefully chosen women with mild symptoms, it may help a little or a fair amount. In others, it does not help at all.

## Who needs it

Nobody strictly needs this treatment. It is something we may discuss when a woman is bothered by:

- a mild feeling of looseness or reduced sensation after childbirth that has not improved with several months of proper pelvic floor exercises;
- vaginal dryness, burning or discomfort during intercourse after menopause, particularly when vaginal oestrogen is unsuitable or not wanted;
- mild leaking of urine on coughing or sneezing, as an add-on to pelvic floor training, after the options are explained.

It is not suitable during pregnancy, with an active vaginal or urinary infection, with unexplained bleeding, or with an abnormal cervical screening result that has not been sorted out. It is not the right treatment for a significant prolapse, and we approach it with extra care in women who have had pelvic radiotherapy or who form thick scars.

> **"My friend had laser and says it changed everything. Will it do the same for me?"**
> It might help you, and it might not. Women start from different places, and their tissues respond differently. What I can promise is an honest examination first, a clear explanation of whether something simpler is likely to work better, and no pressure either way.

## How it is done

At the first visit we talk through your symptoms, your deliveries, your periods or menopause, your medicines and what you hope to change. A gentle examination follows. If your cervical screening is not up to date, we arrange it first.

On the day of treatment:

1. You lie on the examination couch, much as you would for a routine check-up.
2. A numbing cream may be applied to the vaginal opening and left for a short while.
3. A slim, smooth laser probe is placed in the vagina. As it is rotated and gradually withdrawn, it delivers short pulses of laser energy to the vaginal walls. You will feel warmth, and sometimes a light snapping or tingling.
4. If the opening or vulva is also being treated, a separate handpiece is used there.

Sessions are spaced a few weeks apart. The number of sessions is decided after assessment, and we review you each time before going ahead.

## Duration and what to expect on the day

The treatment itself takes about **15 to 30 minutes**, plus time for the numbing cream to work. No injection, spinal or general anaesthesia is needed.

It is an **outpatient procedure**. There is no hospital admission, and you walk out afterwards. Most women drive themselves home, though some prefer company the first time.

## Recovery

**Day 1.** A sense of warmth, mild swelling or tenderness at the opening, and some watery or pinkish discharge are common. Wear a panty liner and loose cotton underwear.

**The first week.** Discharge and light spotting settle over a few days. Most women carry on with work and everyday life normally. Avoid intercourse, tampons, swimming and very hot baths for about a week, or for as long as we advise.

**Weeks 2 to 4.** Any change in moisture or comfort, if it comes, starts to appear gradually as the tissue remodels. The next session, if one is planned, usually falls in this window or soon after.

**By six weeks after the last session.** This is roughly when we can judge how much difference the course has made. Results vary from woman to woman, and any improvement tends to fade with time.

Please call us if you have heavy bleeding, increasing pain, fever, foul-smelling discharge or burning on passing urine that does not settle.

## Risks and complications

Laser is less invasive than surgery, but it is not risk-free. Health regulators, including the US FDA, have warned that energy-based "vaginal rejuvenation" devices have been promoted for uses that are not well established, and have received reports of burns, scarring and long-term pain. You should know about these before you decide.

- **Temporary discomfort**, warmth, swelling, discharge and spotting are common and usually settle within days.
- **Infection**, including thrush or a urinary infection, is uncommon and treatable.
- **Burns, blistering or scarring** are rare, and are less likely when the settings are chosen carefully for your tissue.
- **Pain during intercourse**, usually temporary, rarely longer-lasting.
- **No noticeable improvement.** This is a real possibility, and you should go in knowing it.

> **"Is it safe if I had a caesarean or stitches after delivery?"**
> Usually, yes, once everything has fully healed. We check any scar carefully at the examination and adjust the treatment around it. If something about your tissue makes laser a poor idea, I will tell you so.

## Alternatives

For most women, at least one of these comes **before** laser, not after it.

**Pelvic floor rehabilitation.** For looseness and leaking after childbirth, supervised pelvic floor exercises have the strongest evidence and cost nothing but time. Our article on [vaginal laxity after childbirth, pelvic floor rehab and when a procedure is reasonable](/after-childbirth-vaginal-laxity-pelvic-floor-rehab-and-when-a-procedure-is-reasonable/) explains how to start.

**Moisturisers, lubricants and vaginal oestrogen.** For dryness and painful intercourse after menopause, regular vaginal moisturisers and, where suitable, low-dose vaginal oestrogen are well-studied first options. We cover these in [vaginal dryness and painful intimacy after menopause](/vaginal-dryness-and-painful-intimacy-after-menopause-what-actually-helps/).

**Treatment for leaking urine.** Physiotherapy, bladder training and, when needed, established surgical options are described in our post on [stress urinary incontinence](/leaking-when-you-cough-laugh-or-sneeze-stress-urinary-incontinence/).

**Surgery.** Where there is a true structural problem, such as a prolapse or a poorly healed perineum, surgical repair is the established treatment. If your concern is the shape or size of the labia, [labiaplasty](/treatments/labiaplasty/) is the relevant procedure, not laser.

## Preparing for your treatment

- Book the session for a day when you are not on your period.
- Make sure your cervical screening is up to date; if it is not, we will do it first.
- Tell us if you might be pregnant, or if you have any discharge, itching or burning on passing urine, so an infection can be treated beforehand.
- Mention any previous pelvic surgery, radiotherapy, tendency to scar, cold sores or genital herpes, and all the medicines you take.
- Avoid intercourse and vaginal products for a day or two before, unless we advise otherwise.
- Keep panty liners and loose cotton underwear ready, and plan a quiet first evening.
- Bring your questions written down. No question is too small or too personal.
