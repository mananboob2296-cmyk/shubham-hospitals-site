## What it is

Hysteroscopic tubal cannulation is a small procedure to open a fallopian tube that is blocked at the point where it joins the womb. Doctors call this a proximal or cornual block. Using a hysteroscope, a thin telescope passed through the cervix, we find the tiny opening of the tube inside the womb and guide a fine, soft catheter and wire through it to clear the blockage. There is no cut for the cannulation itself.

It is part of the work of our [advanced laparoscopy and hysteroscopy department](/treatments/advanced-laparoscopy-hysteroscopy/). If you would like to understand the telescope examination of the womb first, read our page on [diagnostic and operative hysteroscopy](/treatments/diagnostic-operative-hysteroscopy/) or our simple guide, [what is hysteroscopy](/what-is-hysteroscopy-a-simple-guide-for-women-in-amravati/). You can also [see all the procedures we perform](/procedures/).

## Who needs it

The fallopian tube is where the egg and sperm meet. If the tube is closed at the womb end, they cannot. Sometimes the cause is a small plug of mucus or debris, sometimes a thin film of scar tissue left by an old infection.

We consider tubal cannulation when:

- a dye test (HSG) or scan shows one or both tubes blocked at the womb end;
- the rest of the tube is believed to be healthy;
- you hope to conceive naturally or with IUI, and would like to give your own tubes a fair chance before IVF;
- a block is found during a [diagnostic hystero-laparoscopy](/treatments/diagnostic-hystero-laparoscopy/) and can be dealt with in the same sitting.

It is not the right choice for everyone. Cannulation does not help when the block is at the far end of the tube, when the tube is swollen and filled with fluid, or when the tubes have been badly damaged by infection, tuberculosis or earlier surgery. It is also not the best use of time when age or the semen report makes IVF the more sensible route.

> **"My HSG says both tubes are blocked. Is IVF my only option?"**
> Not always. A block at the womb end on a dye test is quite often not a true block. The tube can go into spasm during the test, or a small plug may be sitting at the opening. That is why I like to confirm the finding before deciding anything. If the block is real and the rest of the tube looks healthy, cannulation is worth trying. If it is not, I will tell you plainly that IVF is the better use of your time.

## How it is done

The procedure is planned for the days just after your period ends, when the womb lining is thin and the openings of the tubes are easiest to see.

Once you are asleep, the hysteroscope is passed through the cervix and the womb is gently filled with fluid. We look at the whole cavity first, then find the two small openings of the tubes at the upper corners of the womb.

A fine catheter is guided into the blocked opening. Through it, a very soft, flexible wire is advanced gently along the first part of the tube to clear the block. The wire is then withdrawn and a coloured dye is flushed through the catheter.

In most women we combine this with a laparoscopy. A telescope placed through a small cut near the navel watches the tube from the outside. When blue dye spills from the far end of the tube, we know it is open. The laparoscope also lets us watch the wire as it travels, which makes the procedure safer, and lets us check the pelvis for endometriosis or adhesions that could be treated at the same time.

If the wire meets firm resistance, we stop. Forcing it does more harm than good, and a tube that will not open gently is telling us something useful. Both tubes can be treated in the same sitting.

## Anaesthesia, duration and hospital stay

The procedure is done under **general anaesthesia**.

The cannulation itself takes about **15 to 30 minutes**. With a laparoscopy alongside, the whole procedure takes roughly **45 minutes to an hour**.

Most women go home **the same day or the next morning**. Someone should take you home, and you should not drive yourself.

## Recovery

**Day 1.** Period-like cramps and light spotting or a watery, slightly blue-stained discharge from the dye. If you also had a laparoscopy, the small cuts will be sore and you may feel shoulder-tip pain from the gas, which fades. You will be eating and walking within hours.

**The first week.** Cramping and spotting settle over a few days. Simple painkillers are usually enough. Most women are back at desk work in two to five days, depending on whether a laparoscopy was done. Avoid tampons and intercourse for as long as we advise, usually about a week.

**Weeks 2 to 4.** Back to normal activity. In most cases you can start trying for a baby from the next cycle, and we generally encourage you not to put it off.

**By six weeks.** Fully recovered. At the follow-up visit we go through what we found, show you the photographs, and agree on how long to try before considering the next step.

Please call us earlier if you have fever, heavy bleeding, foul-smelling discharge or severe or worsening pain. And when you do conceive, come for an early scan so we can confirm that the pregnancy is in the womb.

## Why keyhole rather than open surgery

In earlier years, a block at the womb end of the tube was treated by an open operation in which the blocked segment was cut out and the tube stitched back to the womb under a microscope. That meant a cut on the abdomen, several days in hospital and weeks of recovery.

Cannulation works from the inside, through the natural passage, with a telescope instead of a cut. Recovery takes days. And if it does not succeed, you have lost very little, because every other option, including IVF, is still open to you.

## Risks and complications

This is a gentle procedure and serious problems are uncommon, but you should know them.

- **Cramping and spotting** for a few days, which is expected.
- **The tube may not open.** Some blocks are too firm or too long, and we will not force them.
- **The tube may block again** in the following months, even after a successful procedure.
- **A small puncture of the tube by the wire.** This is uncommon, is usually seen at once through the laparoscope, and nearly always heals on its own without further treatment.
- **Infection** of the womb or tubes, uncommon and treated with antibiotics.
- **Ectopic pregnancy.** A tube that was once blocked may not work perfectly, so a later pregnancy can occasionally settle in the tube. This is the reason for the early scan. Our page on [ectopic pregnancy surgery](/treatments/laparoscopic-ectopic-pregnancy-surgery/) explains more.
- **Fluid overload** from the fluid used in the womb, rare with careful monitoring.
- **Risks of the laparoscopy**, if one is done: injury to the bowel, bladder or blood vessels is rare.
- **Anaesthetic risks**, which the anaesthetist will discuss with you separately.

> **"If the tube opens, does that mean I will get pregnant?"**
> I wish I could promise that, but I cannot. An open tube gives you a chance that a closed tube does not, and many couples do go on to conceive. But pregnancy also depends on your age, your eggs, the semen report and how healthy the inner lining of the tube is. What I can promise is an honest picture of your chances before you decide.

## Alternatives

**Confirming the block first.** Because a dye test can show a block that is not really there, repeating the test or checking the tubes during a laparoscopy is sometimes all that is needed.

**Trying naturally or with IUI if one tube is open.** One healthy tube is often enough. Our page on [intrauterine insemination](/treatments/intrauterine-insemination/) explains when that helps.

**IVF.** IVF bypasses the tubes completely and is the better choice when both tubes are badly damaged, when the block is at the far end, or when age or sperm quality makes time important. You can read about it on our [IVF and fertility treatments](/treatments/ivf-fertility-treatments/) page.

**Tubal surgery.** Cutting out and rejoining the blocked segment is still possible, but it is rarely chosen today because cannulation and IVF serve most couples better.

## Preparing for your surgery

- Bring your HSG films and report, scans, semen analysis, hormone tests and any earlier treatment records.
- Tell us about allergies, diabetes, thyroid problems, blood thinners, past pelvic infection or tuberculosis, and any previous operations.
- Plan the date with us for just after your period ends.
- Do not eat or drink for the number of hours the anaesthetist advises, usually six to eight.
- Do not use vaginal creams or medicines in the days before unless we have asked you to.
- Arrange for someone to accompany you home, and keep a day or two free to rest.
- Ask your husband or partner to come to the consultation. The decision between cannulation and IVF is one you should make together.
