## What it is

Laparoscopic tubal ligation, also called tubectomy or female sterilisation, is a keyhole operation that blocks both fallopian tubes so that an egg and sperm can no longer meet. It is a method of permanent contraception. The tubes are closed with small clips or rings, sealed with cautery, or, in some cases, a short segment is removed.

Nothing else inside the pelvis is changed. The ovaries stay where they are, keep producing hormones and keep releasing eggs, which the body simply absorbs. This procedure is part of the work of our [advanced laparoscopy and hysteroscopy department](/treatments/advanced-laparoscopy-hysteroscopy/), and you can [see all the procedures we perform](/procedures/).

## Who needs it

This is for women, usually with the support of their partner, who are certain they do not want any more children and want a reliable, long-term method. Typically she has completed her family, or has a medical reason why another pregnancy would be risky for her.

It is also sometimes done at the same time as another operation, for example a caesarean or another laparoscopy, when the decision was made well beforehand.

I say the next part to every woman, and I would like you to read it carefully: this is a decision to make when you are sure, not when you are exhausted, in pain, newly delivered or under pressure from anyone. If there is a real chance you might want another child, ask us about long-acting reversible methods first.

> **"What if my husband or in-laws want it done, but I am not sure?"**
> Then we wait. The decision is yours, and it needs to feel like yours. We are happy to speak with you on your own, and with your partner, as many times as you need. No operation for sterilisation should be done on someone who is hesitant.

## How it is done

You come in fasting, and a pregnancy test is done before the operation. It is best planned in the first half of your cycle, after your period, so that we are sure you are not pregnant.

Under general anaesthesia, a small cut is made near the navel and gas is gently passed into the abdomen. The telescope goes in, and usually one more small port is placed low in the abdomen.

We identify both tubes along their full length, and confirm which structure is which, since this is what the whole operation depends on. Then each tube is blocked, in one of these ways:

- **Clips or rings**, placed on the middle part of the tube. These do the least damage to the tube and are my usual choice in a young woman.
- **Bipolar cautery**, which seals a short segment of the tube.
- **Partial salpingectomy**, removing a short segment, which is the technique we may choose in some situations.

We look at both tubes again to check that the block is in the right place, and the gas is let out. The small cuts are closed with a stitch or skin glue.

## Anaesthesia, duration and hospital stay

The operation is usually done under **general anaesthesia**, which is the most comfortable and the safest way to do it with the telescope.

It generally takes **20 to 30 minutes**, and a little longer if there are adhesions from previous surgery.

Most women go home **the same day or the next morning**, once they are awake, eating and walking.

## Recovery

**Day 1.** Some soreness at the cuts, a bloated feeling, and shoulder-tip pain from the gas, which settles within a day or two. You will be walking within a few hours. A simple painkiller is usually enough.

**The first week.** Mild tiredness and discomfort that eases each day. Showers are fine from day two or three, with the dressings kept dry. Light household tasks and desk work are generally fine.

**Weeks 2 to 4.** Driving and normal activity as you feel able. Avoid heavy lifting and strenuous exercise for about two weeks. Intimacy can resume once you are comfortable. Your next period may be a little different in timing, which is normal.

**By six weeks.** Back to your full routine. Please call us sooner for fever, redness or discharge at a cut, worsening abdominal pain, vomiting, or a missed period.

## Why keyhole rather than open surgery

Compared with the older open "mini-lap" through a cut near the pubic hairline, the keyhole route has smaller cuts, less pain, a shorter stay and a quicker return to normal. It also lets us see the whole pelvis and both tubes, confirm that the correct structures are blocked, and spot any other problem at the same time.

Open or mini-laparotomy sterilisation still has a place, for example in the first couple of days after delivery, or when a keyhole approach is not possible. We choose the route that is best for you.

## Risks and complications

Tubal ligation is a safe, common operation, but it is surgery, and it carries risks:

- **Failure of the method.** A pregnancy after sterilisation is uncommon but possible, and the tube can occasionally open again. If it happens, there is a higher chance that it will be an **ectopic pregnancy**, so an early scan is important. Our page on [laparoscopic ectopic pregnancy surgery](/treatments/laparoscopic-ectopic-pregnancy-surgery/) explains how that is treated.
- **Regret.** This is the most important one. Some women, especially those sterilised when young or after a change in circumstances, later wish they had not. This is why counselling matters so much.
- **Bleeding**, usually minor, from a cut or from the tube.
- **Injury to the bowel, bladder or blood vessels**, uncommon, and a risk of any laparoscopy. If this happens, it may need a larger cut to repair.
- **Infection** at a cut, or rarely within the pelvis.
- **Anaesthetic risks**, which the anaesthetist will discuss with you separately.
- **Conversion to open surgery**, uncommon, if there are dense adhesions or a complication.

It does not protect against infections passed on through sex.

## Alternatives

There are many ways to prevent pregnancy, and it helps to compare them honestly.

**Vasectomy** for the male partner is a shorter, simpler procedure, usually under local anaesthesia, with a lower surgical risk than female sterilisation. It is permanent as well, and it takes a few months before the semen is confirmed clear.

**Long-acting reversible contraception**, such as a hormonal or copper intrauterine device, or an implant. These work for years, are very effective, and can be removed if you change your mind.

**Pills, injections, condoms and other short-term methods**, which suit those who are not yet certain.

If you are close to menopause or have heavy periods, a hormonal coil may even help with both problems. Tell us what matters to you.

## Preparing for your surgery

- Take your time over the decision, and discuss it with your partner. Ask us anything, as often as you need.
- Tell us about your periods, your last menstrual date, and whether you could be pregnant. A pregnancy test is done before surgery.
- Bring your previous surgery and scan reports, and tell us about any allergies, diabetes, thyroid problems, blood thinners or other regular medicines.
- Fast as advised, usually for 6 to 8 hours before the operation.
- Arrange for someone to accompany you home, and plan a couple of lighter days afterwards.
- If you are on an interval contraceptive, such as the pill, ask us when to stop it.
