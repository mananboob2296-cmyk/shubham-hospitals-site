Many of the women I see have quietly rearranged their lives around this. They know where every toilet is. They wear dark clothes, carry a spare pad, skip the jumping game with the children, and brace themselves before a sneeze. Most have never said a word about it, even to a sister or a close friend.

If that sounds familiar, let me say the useful thing first: leaking when you cough, laugh or sneeze is called stress urinary incontinence, it is common, and it can usually be improved a good deal.

## What is actually going on

Your bladder is held shut by a small muscular valve, the urethra, with a hammock of pelvic floor muscles and ligaments underneath it. Every time you cough, laugh, jump or lift, the pressure inside your abdomen shoots up. When the support is strong, the valve stays closed. When the support has weakened, a little urine escapes.

The word "stress" here has nothing to do with worry. It simply means physical strain on the bladder.

Things that commonly weaken that support:

- Pregnancy and vaginal delivery, especially after several children or a long labour
- The fall in oestrogen around and after menopause
- A long-standing cough, such as in smokers or with asthma
- Years of straining with constipation
- Being overweight, or regular heavy lifting
- Some women simply have naturally looser tissue

Having one or more of these does not mean you will leak, and leaking does not mean you did something wrong.

## Is it stress, urge, or both?

A quick self-check before you see a doctor. Tick the ones that sound like you:

- I leak a small amount when I cough, sneeze, laugh, jump or lift
- It happens without any warning and without a strong urge
- I leak on the way to the toilet because the urge came on so suddenly
- I need to go very often, including several times at night
- I feel I cannot hold on once the urge arrives

The first two point towards stress incontinence. The last three point towards an overactive bladder. Many women tick boxes from both lists, and that is useful for your doctor to know, because the treatments differ.

> **"I had my babies twenty years ago and it only started now. Is it still linked?"**
>
> Quite possibly. The tissue is often compensating for years and then gives way when oestrogen falls after menopause. So the delivery and the menopause can both play a part, and the second is partly treatable with local oestrogen. I wrote about that side of things in our post on [vaginal dryness after menopause](/vaginal-dryness-and-painful-intimacy-after-menopause-what-actually-helps/).

## What helps, in the order I usually suggest it

**1. Pelvic floor exercises, done properly.** Imagine you are trying to stop passing wind and, at the same moment, stopping the flow of urine. That gentle inward and upward squeeze is the muscle. Hold for a few seconds, relax fully, and repeat. Keep your thighs, buttocks and tummy soft. Practise daily, and squeeze just before you cough or lift. It needs patience; give it a few months.

**2. A physiotherapist if you are unsure.** It is genuinely easy to squeeze the wrong muscles. A pelvic floor physiotherapist can check, and teach you.

**3. Adjust the everyday things.** Reduce excess caffeine, avoid drinking large amounts at once, and do not "go just in case" every hour, which trains the bladder to hold less. Treat a persistent cough and constipation, and aim for a comfortable weight.

**4. Local oestrogen after menopause.** If the tissue is thin and dry, low-dose vaginal oestrogen can help the urethra and surrounding tissue, and it is prescribed by a doctor after a short check.

**5. Devices and procedures when needed.** A vaginal pessary can support the bladder outlet for some women. If symptoms still trouble you after these steps, there are procedures, such as a sling that supports the urethra or a bulking injection. They suit some women and not others, so we weigh your tests, your general health and any plan for future pregnancies. I would never recommend one before the simpler measures have had a fair trial.

> **"Should I just stop drinking water so I leak less?"**
>
> Please do not. Concentrated urine irritates the bladder and can make urgency worse, and you risk infections and stones. Drink normally through the day, a little less in the couple of hours before bed.

## Myth versus fact

**Myth:** It is a normal part of being a mother, or of getting older.

**Fact:** It is common, but it is not something you have to accept. Treatment works for most women.

**Myth:** Wearing pads every day is the safe solution.

**Fact:** Pads manage the problem; they do not treat it. Use them if you want, but do not stop there.

**Myth:** Surgery is the only real answer.

**Fact:** Exercises and simple measures are tried first, and help many women enough that nothing more is needed.

## What to expect at your appointment

We start with a conversation: when it leaks, how much, what you drink, your deliveries, your periods, any medicines. You may be asked to keep a simple bladder diary for a few days. Then a gentle examination, often with a cough test, to see the leak and check the support. A urine test rules out infection. Sometimes an ultrasound, or a more detailed bladder pressure study, is needed, particularly if surgery is being considered. You will always be told why a test is being done.

## When to come in sooner

Please see a doctor promptly if you notice:

- Blood in the urine
- Burning or pain passing urine, or repeated infections
- Difficulty emptying your bladder, or a very weak stream
- Constant dribbling, day and night
- A bulge or heaviness in the vagina, or new back or leg weakness
