Not long ago, if a mother asked me about the HPV vaccine, the honest answer included a sigh: it works very well, but it is expensive and many families cannot reach it. That has changed. Since February 2026, India's Universal Immunisation Programme includes an HPV vaccine for the first time, given free of charge. This is a real step forward, and I want you to understand it properly, including what the free programme covers, what it does not, and the other schedules your doctor may mention.

## What the free programme offers

India launched its HPV Vaccination Programme on 28 February 2026. Here is what it gives:

- **One dose** of Gardasil-4, a vaccine that protects against HPV types 6, 11, 16 and 18
- **Free**, at government vaccination sites
- For **girls aged 14**. Girls who were about to turn 15 were also included during the launch campaign
- Now offered on routine immunisation days, with registration on the U-WIN portal

At the site, the girl is observed for 30 minutes after the injection, and she should not come on an empty stomach. The programme works only through government centres that have a cold-chain point and a medical officer ready to handle any reaction.

Why does this matter so much? India sees more than 1,20,000 new cervical cancer cases and about 80,000 deaths a year (GLOBOCAN 2022), roughly a quarter of the world's total. HPV types 16 and 18 cause over 80% of cases here. A vaccine that reaches every 14-year-old, whatever her family's income, can change that over time.

## Is one dose enough?

> **"My neighbour's daughter got three doses. Is one dose a half-measure?"**
>
> No. In 2022 the WHO's expert advisory group reviewed the evidence and concluded that a single dose gives protection comparable to two doses in girls aged 9 to 20. More than 90 countries already use single-dose schedules. The government reports 93–100% effectiveness against the types the vaccine covers. I am comfortable telling families that the free single dose is a genuine, evidence-backed option.

## The standard schedules, side by side

Single-dose is not the only licensed approach. The traditional schedules are still valid, and some parents, or their doctors, prefer them. Here they are in plain terms:

| Age at first dose | Standard schedule |
|---|---|
| 9 to 14 years | Two doses, at 0 and 6 months |
| 15 years and above, or a weakened immune system | Three doses, at 0, 1 to 2 months, and 6 months |

So what should you do? There is no single right answer. The free single dose is the easiest route and fully backed by WHO guidance. Some families like the reassurance of the longer schedule, and a girl with a weakened immune system needs three doses regardless. Both choices are honest ones, and I would rather you decide calmly after a conversation with your doctor than feel pushed either way.

## A few questions I hear every week

> **"Does the vaccine make girls start early with relationships?"**
>
> There is no evidence of that. Think of it the way you think of the polio or hepatitis B vaccine: it is protection given before the risk arrives, which is exactly when it works best.

> **"Is it safe?"**
>
> Mild soreness at the injection site, a little fever or tiredness are the common effects, and they pass in a day or two. That is why the observation period exists: in the very rare case of an allergic reaction, help is right there.

## Who should wait or skip it

The vaccine is not given in these situations, and your doctor will check:

- Pregnancy
- Allergy to yeast, or a previous allergic reaction to a vaccine
- A moderate or severe illness on the day (she can come back once recovered)
- Anyone who has already received an HPV vaccine
- Girls outside the target age, for the free programme

## What about older girls and women?

The free programme does not offer catch-up vaccination, and this is where many women ask me, "Is it too late?" Not necessarily. The vaccine works best when given before any exposure to HPV, but many women in their teens and twenties, and some beyond, can still benefit, and your doctor will look at your age, history and circumstances. It is then a private decision. Please remember two things: vaccination does not treat an infection already present, and it does not replace cervical screening. Our post on [cervical cancer screening](/cervical-cancer-screening-pap-hpv-test-and-when-to-start/) explains when to start.

**A quick checklist before you decide:**

- How old is your daughter, and is she in the free programme's age group?
- Has she had any HPV vaccine before?
- Any history of vaccine allergy, or any condition that weakens immunity?
- Do you want the single-dose route, or would you like to discuss a longer schedule?
- Do you know the nearest government vaccination site and the U-WIN registration steps?
