A woman once rang me on a Sunday evening, voice shaking, because she had opened her lab report online and seen the words "atypical squamous cells". She had read two paragraphs on the internet and was already planning the worst. I understand that completely. Medical language is not written for the person holding the report. So let me translate it, the way I do across my desk.

First, the most important sentence in this article: **an abnormal Pap result is not a diagnosis of cancer.** It is a signal that some cells deserve a closer look, which is exactly what screening is for. If you have not read about the test itself, our post on [cervical cancer screening](/cervical-cancer-screening-pap-hpv-test-and-when-to-start/) covers who needs it and when.

## What the words on your report actually mean

Pap reports use a standard vocabulary (the Bethesda system). Here is the plain-language version.

**Normal, or "negative for intraepithelial lesion".** Nothing worrying was seen. Carry on with your routine screening schedule.

**"Inflammation" or "reactive changes".** This is the one that worries women most and deserves it least. Inflammation simply means the cervix looked irritated. Common causes include a vaginal infection, a coil or other device, dryness after menopause, or recent intercourse before the test. It is not a pre-cancer. We treat an infection if there is one and, depending on your history, may repeat the test.

**ASCUS (atypical squamous cells of undetermined significance).** A few cells looked slightly unusual, but not clearly abnormal. "Undetermined significance" is honest wording: the pathologist could not say more. Often we test the same sample for high-risk HPV. If it is negative, you generally go back to routine screening. If it is positive, we usually look at the cervix with a magnifying instrument.

**LSIL (low-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion).** Mild changes, most often the visible footprint of an HPV infection. Many of these settle by themselves, especially in younger women. Depending on your age and other results, we either repeat the test after a set interval or perform colposcopy.

**HSIL (high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion) and ASC-H.** More marked changes, or changes where we cannot rule out a higher grade. These are the ones we take seriously, not because they are cancer, but because they are the stage at which treatment can stop cancer from ever forming. Colposcopy with a small biopsy is the usual next step.

**AGC (atypical glandular cells).** These come from glands in the cervical canal or sometimes the uterus. They are less common, and we investigate them a little more broadly, which can include looking at the womb lining in women over a certain age.

> **"Doctor, if it's only mild, why can't we just ignore it?"**
>
> Often, with mild changes, watching is exactly the right plan, and we do not rush to treat. But "watching" means a scheduled repeat test, not forgetting. The women who run into trouble are the ones who never go back.

## What happens next: colposcopy, step by step

If your doctor advises colposcopy, here is what to expect. It is done in the clinic, usually in 10 to 15 minutes.

1. **Same position as a Pap test.** A speculum gently opens the vagina so the cervix can be seen.
2. **A closer look.** A magnifying instrument sits outside the body. Nothing goes inside beyond the speculum.
3. **A mild solution.** Dilute vinegar-like liquid is applied to the cervix. It may sting slightly and makes abnormal areas easier to see.
4. **A tiny biopsy, if needed.** If an area looks suspicious, a very small piece is taken. You may feel a pinch or short cramp. Light spotting for a few days afterwards is common.
5. **Results.** The biopsy report usually comes back within about a week or two, and we go through it with you in person.

If the biopsy shows higher-grade change (often called CIN 2 or CIN 3), the usual treatment is a short procedure that removes the affected area of the cervix. Your doctor will explain which option suits you, and whether you are planning a pregnancy matters to that choice.

**Before you go in, a quick checklist:**

- Book the visit when you are not menstruating
- Avoid intercourse, vaginal creams and douching for a day or two beforehand
- Bring your previous reports, including old Pap or HPV results
- Tell us if you might be pregnant or are trying to conceive
- Write down your questions; people forget them in the room

## Myth versus fact

**Myth:** An abnormal Pap means I have cancer.

**Fact:** The great majority of abnormal results are pre-cancerous or minor changes, not cancer. Catching them early is the whole point.

**Myth:** It means I did something wrong, or my partner did.

**Fact:** HPV is extremely common and can stay quiet for years. An abnormal result says nothing reliable about when or from whom it was acquired.

**Myth:** If I feel fine, I can skip the follow-up.

**Fact:** Cervical changes cause no symptoms in their early stages. Feeling fine is exactly why follow-up is easy to skip, and exactly why it matters.

**Myth:** The HPV vaccine makes my abnormal result irrelevant.

**Fact:** Vaccination protects against future infection with the types it covers. It does not treat changes already present. If you want to know more about it, see our guide to [the HPV vaccine](/the-hpv-vaccine-the-free-single-dose-the-standard-schedule-and-how-to-choose/).

## When not to wait for your next scheduled test

Come in sooner if you notice bleeding between periods, bleeding after intercourse, bleeding after menopause, or an unusual discharge that does not settle. These are worth checking regardless of what your last Pap said.
