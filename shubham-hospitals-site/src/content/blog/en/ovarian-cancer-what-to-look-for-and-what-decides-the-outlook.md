Ovarian cancer has an unfair reputation as the cancer with no warning. That is not quite right. It does give hints, but they are the sort of hints every woman gets from time to time: a bloated tummy, a heavy lunch that will not go down, a bladder that suddenly seems impatient. Because these are so ordinary, they are easy to explain away, by a woman and sometimes by her doctor too.

I am not writing this to make you scan every bloated evening. I am writing so that you know which pattern is worth a visit, and what happens if you do come.

## The symptom cluster to know

The ovaries sit deep in the pelvis, so early changes are quiet. The pattern that should make you pause is a **new cluster that persists**:

- Bloating or a swollen belly that does not come and go with meals or periods
- Feeling full quickly, or little appetite
- Needing to pass urine more often or more urgently, without an infection
- Pelvic or lower abdominal pressure or aching
- Changes in bowel habit that are new for you
- Unexplained tiredness or back ache

Any one of these is usually something simple. It is the combination, lasting more than two to three weeks, that I would like to examine. Bloating that is new, daily, and not explained by food is the one I take most seriously.

> **"I have had gas and bloating for years. Is that a worry?"**

Long-standing, unchanging bloating is rarely the concern. The question is whether something has *changed*: more frequent, more constant, or joined by early fullness or urinary urgency. A change is the signal, not the symptom alone.

## Who is at higher risk

Most women who develop ovarian cancer have no obvious risk factor, so please do not read this list as a verdict. It is still useful to know:

- Age, with most cases occurring after menopause
- A family history of ovarian, breast, or bowel and womb cancer, especially at a young age
- Inherited gene changes such as BRCA1, BRCA2, or the Lynch syndrome genes
- Endometriosis, and never having carried a pregnancy
- Long use of menopausal hormone therapy

Pregnancies, breastfeeding, and use of the contraceptive pill are associated with a lower risk. If several close relatives have had these cancers, tell us. Genetic counselling is a calm, practical conversation, and it can protect your sisters and daughters as well as you.

## Myth versus fact

**Myth:** "My Pap smear is normal, so my ovaries are fine." **Fact:** A Pap smear checks only the cervix. We explain what it does and does not do in [our guide to cervical screening](/cervical-cancer-screening-pap-hpv-test-and-when-to-start/).

**Myth:** "A cyst on the scan means cancer." **Fact:** The vast majority of ovarian cysts are benign. Our post on [ovarian cysts](/ovarian-cysts-when-to-worry-and-when-to-wait/) covers the types and when to watch.

**Myth:** "A normal CA-125 means I am clear." **Fact:** CA-125 can be normal in early cancer and raised in harmless conditions like endometriosis, fibroids, or even a period. It helps interpret a scan; it is not a stand-alone test.

## What to expect at your appointment

We begin by listening: when the symptoms started, how they have changed, your periods, your family history. A gentle abdominal and pelvic examination follows. The key test is a transvaginal ultrasound, which shows the size of the ovaries and whether any swelling is plain fluid or has solid or irregular parts. Sometimes a CT or MRI scan and a blood test such as CA-125 are added. Taken together, they let us sort a harmless cyst from one that needs more attention.

> **"What if the doctors find something suspicious?"**

Then we plan carefully rather than quickly. The diagnosis is made on tissue, usually obtained at surgery, and the surgery itself is part of the treatment. We talk through every step before anything is decided, and nothing is hurried past you.

## What actually decides the outlook

I will not quote you a percentage, because numbers describe large groups and you are one person. What I can tell you are the things that shape the picture:

- **Stage:** how far the disease has spread when it is found.
- **Grade and histology:** the type of cancer cell and how aggressive it looks under the microscope. Ovarian cancer is not one disease.
- **Completeness of surgery:** how thoroughly all visible disease can be removed matters a great deal.
- **Response to platinum-based chemotherapy:** most women are advised chemotherapy after surgery, and how the cancer responds guides what comes next.
- **Genetic findings:** BRCA and related results can open up additional treatment options.

Treatment usually combines surgery with chemotherapy, and sometimes targeted medicines. Details of the surgical side are on our [gynaecologic oncology](/treatments/gynecologic-oncology/) page. If you want to understand how the same clinic approaches cancer of the womb lining, read about [the warning signs of endometrial cancer](/endometrial-cancer-the-warning-signs-women-dismiss/).

## Do not sit on a symptom

If you have had bloating, early fullness, urinary urgency, or pelvic pressure for more than a few weeks, please get it looked at. Most of the time we will find something simple and you will go home reassured. On the rare occasion we do not, an earlier start gives everyone more options.
