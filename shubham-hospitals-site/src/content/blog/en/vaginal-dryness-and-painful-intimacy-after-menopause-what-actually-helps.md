Most women who come to me about this do not start with it. They mention a "burning" they put down to soap, or recurrent urine infections, or just say they have "gone off" intimacy. Then, near the end of the consultation, with one hand already on the bag, they say it quietly: it hurts, and I do not know who to ask.

If that is you, I want you to hear this first. It is common, it has a medical name, and it is one of the more treatable problems I see.

## What is actually happening

Before menopause, oestrogen keeps the lining of the vagina thick, moist and stretchy. When oestrogen falls, that lining thins out, produces less natural lubrication, and loses some of its elasticity. The same change affects the urethra and bladder area, which is why dryness often travels with urinary urgency, stinging, or repeated infections.

The whole cluster is now called genitourinary syndrome of menopause. The name matters because it tells you this is not "just ageing" or something in your head. It is a physical change with physical treatments.

Common signs include:

- Dryness, itching or a raw, burning feeling
- Pain or tearing during intimacy, or light spotting afterwards
- Needing to pass urine more often, or recurrent urine infections
- A feeling of tightness, or discomfort when sitting or exercising

Unlike hot flushes, this does not usually fade with time. Left alone it tends to creep up, so earlier help is easier help.

> **"I am 58. Surely it is too late to bother about this?"**
>
> Not at all. I have treated women in their sixties and seventies who were told to simply accept it. The tissue responds to treatment at almost any age, and relief often comes within a few weeks. Your comfort matters at every age.

## What genuinely helps, step by step

**1. Start with a moisturiser, not just a lubricant.** These are different things. A lubricant is for the moment of intimacy. A vaginal moisturiser is used regularly, usually two or three times a week, to keep the tissue hydrated day to day. Choose fragrance-free products, and avoid anything scented, "warming" or sold as a wash.

**2. Use a good lubricant when you need one.** Water-based or silicone-based lubricants are the usual choices. Oils can damage condoms, so check if that is relevant to you.

**3. Ask about low-dose vaginal oestrogen.** This is the treatment that addresses the cause. It comes as a cream, a small tablet or a ring, and the dose is tiny compared with menopausal hormone tablets, with very little absorbed into the bloodstream. For most women it is the most effective option. It needs a prescription and a short check of your history first.

**4. Keep the tissue gently active.** Regular intimacy, with plenty of time, lubricant and no goal beyond comfort, helps keep the tissue supple. So can a pelvic floor physiotherapist if the muscles have tensed up in anticipation of pain, which is very common.

**5. Look after the other things that make it worse.** Smoking, harsh soaps and some medicines can add to dryness. If you take a medicine you suspect is contributing, do not stop it yourself; mention it to your doctor.

> **"A friend told me about laser treatment for this. Is it better?"**
>
> I would be careful. The evidence for vaginal laser in menopausal dryness is still limited compared with moisturisers and vaginal oestrogen, which have been studied for much longer. It is not a first step. If you are curious, ask your doctor to explain honestly who might benefit and who might not, before spending money on it.

## Myth versus fact

**Myth:** Dryness means I have lost interest in my partner.

**Fact:** Pain makes anyone avoid intimacy. Treating the pain often brings the interest back.

**Myth:** Using vaginal oestrogen is the same as taking HRT tablets.

**Fact:** The dose and the way it works in the body are quite different. It acts mainly where it is applied.

**Myth:** It is embarrassing, so I should manage on my own.

**Fact:** We see this every week. Nothing you describe will surprise us.

## When to come in sooner

Dryness is common, but some things need a proper check rather than a moisturiser. Please see a doctor promptly if you have:

- Any bleeding after menopause, even a small amount, which always needs to be looked into
- A foul-smelling or blood-stained discharge
- Pelvic pain that is not linked to intimacy
- A lump, sore or patch that does not heal
