"Doctor, my periods have always been like this." I hear that sentence almost every week. Usually it comes from a woman who changes her pad every hour on day two, carries a spare set of clothes to the office, and has stopped wearing light-coloured kurtas altogether. She isn't complaining. She has simply decided this is what periods are.

It doesn't have to be. Heavy menstrual bleeding is one of the most common reasons women see a gynaecologist, and it is one of the most treatable. The hard part is often recognising that your "normal" is not normal.

## So how much is too much?

Nobody measures their blood loss in millilitres, and we don't expect you to. What matters is how the bleeding affects your life. Run through this quick self-check:

- You change a pad or tampon every one to two hours on your heaviest days
- You use two pads together, or a pad with a menstrual cup, just to feel safe
- You wake at night to change, or stain the bedsheet despite precautions
- You pass clots larger than a one-rupee coin
- Your period lasts more than seven days
- You avoid travel, functions or work on heavy days
- You feel tired, breathless or dizzy, especially towards the end of your period

If two or three of these sound familiar, your bleeding is heavy enough to deserve a proper look.

> **"Are clots dangerous?"**
>
> Small clots on the heaviest day are common. Repeatedly passing large clots simply tells us the flow is faster than your body can keep liquid. It isn't an emergency in itself, but it is a sign of heavy bleeding worth investigating.

## The hidden cost: anaemia

This is the part I worry about most. A woman can lose more iron every month than her diet replaces, and the drop happens so slowly that she adapts to it. She thinks the exhaustion is work, children, age. Then a routine blood test shows a haemoglobin far below where it should be.

Hair fall, breathlessness on the stairs, cold hands, poor concentration and a pale face are all possible clues. Treating heavy periods is not only about the bleeding; it is about giving back the energy anaemia has quietly taken away.

## What causes heavy periods?

Often there is a clear reason; sometimes there isn't, and that is fine too. The common causes are:

- **Fibroids**, especially those that bulge into the cavity of the uterus. Our [fibroids guide](/uterine-fibroids-do-they-always-need-surgery/) covers these in detail.
- **Adenomyosis**, where the lining grows into the muscle wall, typically causing heavy *and* painful periods. See [how adenomyosis differs from fibroids](/adenomyosis-why-it-gets-missed-and-how-it-differs-from-fibroids/).
- **Endometrial polyps**, small soft growths of the lining.
- **Hormonal imbalance**, when ovulation is irregular, as in PCOS, thyroid problems, or the years leading up to menopause. Our post on [irregular periods](/irregular-periods-common-causes-and-when-to-worry/) explains this side.
- **Medicines and devices**, such as blood thinners or a copper IUD.
- **Bleeding disorders**, worth considering if heavy periods started from your very first cycle, or you bruise easily or bleed long after a tooth extraction.
- **Thickening of the lining (endometrial hyperplasia)**, and rarely cancer, which is why bleeding changes after about 45 always need checking.

If pain rather than flow is your main problem, our article on [painful periods](/why-are-my-periods-so-painful-a-gynecologist-explains/) is the better starting point.

## Myth vs fact

**Myth:** Heavy bleeding "cleans out" the body, so it is healthy.
**Fact:** Menstrual blood is not toxic waste. Losing too much only depletes your iron.

**Myth:** If you have had children, heavy periods are expected.
**Fact:** Childbirth does not make heavy bleeding normal. A cause is often found and treated.

**Myth:** The only real fix is removing the uterus.
**Fact:** Hysterectomy is one option among many, and usually the last one we discuss.

## What to expect at your appointment

I start by asking about your cycle, how many pads you use, how long the bleeding lasts, whether you want children, and what medicines you take. Then a gentle examination and a pelvic ultrasound, usually transvaginal, to look at the uterus, its lining and the ovaries.

A blood test checks haemoglobin, and often thyroid function. Depending on your age and what the scan shows, we may suggest a sample of the lining or a [hysteroscopy](/treatments/diagnostic-operative-hysteroscopy/), where a thin camera looks directly inside the uterus. Many polyps can be removed during that same procedure.

> **"Do I need all these tests?"**
>
> Not always. A young woman with no other symptoms may need only a blood test and a scan. The work-up is matched to you, not run as a fixed package.

## Treatment: a wide range of choices

The right option depends on the cause, your age, and whether you are planning a pregnancy.

- **Non-hormonal medicines** such as tranexamic acid, which reduces blood loss, and anti-inflammatory painkillers. Both are taken only during the period.
- **Hormonal tablets**, including certain contraceptive pills or progestogens, to regulate and lighten the cycle.
- **A hormone-releasing coil (LNG-IUS)**, placed inside the uterus, which thins the lining and lightens bleeding considerably for many women while also working as contraception.
- **Iron supplements**, by mouth or sometimes by injection, to rebuild your stores. This step is as important as stopping the bleeding.
- **Treating a specific cause**, for example removing a polyp by hysteroscopy or fibroids by [laparoscopic myomectomy](/treatments/laparoscopic-myomectomy/), keeping the uterus for women who want it.
- **Hysterectomy**, for women whose family is complete and whose symptoms have not settled with other treatment. When it is needed it can often be done by keyhole surgery; see our [total laparoscopic hysterectomy page](/treatments/total-laparoscopic-hysterectomy/).

> **"Will the medicines affect my chances of getting pregnant later?"**
>
> The commonly used options are reversible. Fertility usually returns once tablets are stopped or a coil is removed. We always factor your pregnancy plans into the choice.

## When to seek help the same day

Go to a hospital promptly if you are soaking through a pad every hour for several hours in a row, feel faint or breathless, have a racing heartbeat, or could be pregnant and are bleeding heavily.
