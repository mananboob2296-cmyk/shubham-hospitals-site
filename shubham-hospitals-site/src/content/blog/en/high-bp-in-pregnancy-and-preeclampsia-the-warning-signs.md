Every time you come in for a pregnancy check-up, someone wraps a cuff around your arm before anything else happens. Many women treat it as a formality, the bit you get through before the scan or the conversation. I'd like you to see it differently. That small reading is one of the most useful things we measure in pregnancy, because the condition it watches for, preeclampsia, very often starts without a single symptom.

## What preeclampsia actually is

Some women already have high blood pressure before they conceive. Others develop raised BP for the first time in pregnancy, usually after 20 weeks; this is called gestational hypertension. Preeclampsia is the step beyond that: high blood pressure together with signs that it's affecting other parts of the body, most often protein leaking into the urine, but sometimes changes in the liver, kidneys, blood platelets, or the baby's growth.

The root of it lies in the placenta, and the only true cure is delivering the baby and placenta. That doesn't mean every woman with preeclampsia needs an immediate delivery. It means the whole plan revolves around keeping mother and baby safe while weighing how far along the pregnancy is.

In general terms, a BP of 140/90 or above after 20 weeks is treated as raised. Readings of 160/110 or higher are considered severe and need urgent treatment.

## The warning signs to act on the same day

This is the part I'd ask you to save on your phone or stick on the fridge. If you're more than 20 weeks pregnant, or have delivered within the last six weeks, come to the hospital that same day, not at your next appointment, if you have:

- A severe headache that doesn't go away with rest or a simple painkiller
- Blurred vision, flashing lights, spots in front of your eyes, or sensitivity to light
- Pain just below the ribs, especially on the right side or in the upper middle of the abdomen, which can feel like severe acidity
- Sudden swelling of your face, hands or around your eyes
- Nausea or vomiting that starts suddenly in the second half of pregnancy
- Breathlessness, or feeling unusually unwell and "not right"
- Your baby moving less than usual

A fit (seizure), fainting or confusion is an emergency: call for help immediately.

> **"I just have a headache. Isn't it a bit dramatic to rush in?"**
>
> Most headaches in pregnancy turn out to be ordinary ones, and I'm always happy to send a mother home reassured. But a headache that's stronger than usual, won't settle, or comes with visual changes is exactly the kind preeclampsia can cause. A BP check and a urine test take a few minutes. That's a very small price for an answer.

## Who's more likely to develop it?

Preeclampsia can happen to anyone, including women who were perfectly healthy before pregnancy. Some situations raise the chances: a first pregnancy, preeclampsia in an earlier pregnancy, high blood pressure, diabetes or kidney disease before pregnancy, autoimmune conditions such as lupus, twins, pregnancy after IVF, being over 40, a high BMI, or a mother or sister who had it.

If any of these apply to you, tell us at your first visit. For women at higher risk, doctors often advise low-dose aspirin started in early pregnancy, as it has been shown to lower the chance of preeclampsia. Please don't start it on your own; the decision, timing and dose should come from your obstetrician. You'll find more on how we look after these pregnancies in our guide to [high-risk pregnancy](/high-risk-pregnancy-what-it-means-and-how-to-stay-safe/).

## Myth versus fact

**Myth: "If I feel fine, my BP must be fine."** Fact: high BP in pregnancy is usually silent. That's precisely why it's checked at every visit.

**Myth: "Swollen feet mean preeclampsia."** Fact: swollen ankles by evening are common and usually harmless. Sudden swelling, puffiness of the face or hands, or swelling with a headache are what count.

**Myth: "Eating less salt will prevent it."** Fact: a sensible diet is good for you, but cutting salt hasn't been shown to prevent preeclampsia. Regular checks, and aspirin where advised, are what make the difference.

**Myth: "Once the baby is born, it's over."** Fact: preeclampsia can appear or worsen after delivery, so keep watching for the warning signs for six weeks.

## What happens if your BP is high

> **"Will I need to be admitted straight away?"**
>
> Not always. It depends on how high the readings are, what your urine and blood tests show, and how your baby is doing.

When we find a raised reading, we first repeat it after you've rested, then check your urine for protein and send blood tests for kidney function, liver function and platelets. We look at the baby too, with a scan of growth and fluid and often Doppler studies of blood flow; our [pregnancy scans guide](/pregnancy-scans-explained-which-scan-when-why-in-amravati/) explains these. Some women can be monitored as outpatients with frequent visits and BP tablets that are safe in pregnancy. Others are better looked after in hospital, where we can watch closely, give medicine to protect against seizures if needed, and give steroid injections to help the baby's lungs if an early delivery looks likely.

The timing and route of delivery are decided case by case. Preeclampsia doesn't automatically mean a caesarean; many women have a normal delivery.

## Checking your BP at home

If your doctor asks you to monitor at home, a validated automatic upper-arm machine works well. Sit quietly for five minutes, feet flat, arm supported at heart level, and write down each reading with the date and time. Bring the record to every visit. If a reading is 140/90 or more, rest and check again; if it's still high, call us. If it's 160/110 or more, or you have any warning sign, come in straight away.

## After delivery, and later in life

Your BP will be checked in the days after birth and at your postnatal visit, and some women need to keep taking tablets for a few weeks. Having had preeclampsia also tells us something about your long-term health, so it's worth having your BP checked at least once a year afterwards.
