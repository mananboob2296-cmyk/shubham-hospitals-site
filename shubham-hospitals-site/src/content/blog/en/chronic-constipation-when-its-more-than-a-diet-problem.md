By the time most people come to me with constipation, they have already tried everything the family suggested. Papaya in the morning. Warm water with lemon. Isabgol at night. A teaspoon of ghee. And for a while some of it helped, until it didn't.

That is usually the moment to stop experimenting and start asking a different question. Not "what else can I eat?" but "why is my bowel behaving like this in the first place?"

## First, what counts as constipation

Doctors don't define constipation by whether you go every morning. Healthy people range from a few motions a day to a few a week. Constipation means stools that are hard or lumpy, a lot of straining, a feeling that something is still left behind, or going much less often than is normal for you. When this has gone on for a few months, we call it chronic.

Diet and water genuinely do matter, and for many people a gradual increase in fibre, enough fluids and a bit more walking is the whole answer. This post is for the people for whom it isn't.

> **"Doctor, I eat plenty of vegetables and drink three litres of water. Why am I still constipated?"**

Because fibre and water only fix one kind of constipation, the kind where the stool itself is too dry and small. If the bowel is moving slowly for another reason, or the muscles at the exit aren't working together, more salad won't solve it. That's not a failure on your part. It just means we need to look further.

## The causes that hide behind "just constipation"

**Medicines.** This is the one I check first, because it is common and easy to miss. Iron and calcium tablets, some painkillers, certain antacids, some blood pressure tablets and some medicines for mood or sleep can all slow the bowel. Please don't stop anything on your own; bring the list and we will go through it together.

**Hormones and metabolism.** An underactive thyroid, diabetes and a high calcium level can all slow the gut. Pregnancy does it too, through hormones and the growing uterus. A simple blood test often settles these questions.

**An exit that won't open properly.** Some people have a stool ready to pass but can't get it out. The pelvic floor muscles, which should relax during a motion, tighten instead. People describe pushing hard with little result, needing to press around the back passage, or sitting for a very long time. This is called obstructed defecation or dyssynergia, and it responds to targeted pelvic floor retraining rather than to more fibre.

**A slow-moving bowel.** In some people the whole colon moves stool along slowly. They may go a week without any urge at all. Fibre can actually make bloating worse here, and the treatment is different.

**Irritable bowel syndrome.** When constipation comes with cramps and bloating that ease after a motion, IBS is often part of the picture.

**A narrowing in the bowel.** This is the less common cause, and the one people worry about most. A growth or a narrowing can change bowel habits, which is why the warning signs below matter.

**Holding it in.** A painful [anal fissure](/anal-fissure-why-it-hurts-so-much-and-how-it-heals-without-surgery/) or swollen [piles](/are-piles-serious-when-you-should-see-a-surgeon/) can make people put off going, which turns into a cycle. Long-standing straining can also worsen piles. I've covered both in those posts, so I won't repeat them here.

## A quick self-check: signs that need investigation

Constipation is very rarely dangerous, but please see a doctor rather than self-treating if you notice any of these:

- Blood in or on the stool, or black, tarry stools
- Weight loss you can't explain, or loss of appetite
- A new, persistent change in your bowel habit in middle age or later, especially past 50
- Tiredness or breathlessness that might point to anaemia
- A family history of bowel cancer or inflammatory bowel disease
- Stools that have stayed much narrower than usual
- Symptoms that wake you at night

And one situation that should not wait for an appointment: **severe belly pain with a swollen abdomen and vomiting, particularly if you can't pass stool or gas.** That needs to be seen the same day.

## What to expect at your appointment

We will talk first: how long this has been going on, what your stools are like, how much you strain, what medicines you take, and what you've already tried. Then a gentle examination of the abdomen, and usually of the back passage, which tells us a surprising amount about the muscles at the exit. Most people find it far less uncomfortable than they feared.

Depending on what we find, we may suggest blood tests for haemoglobin, thyroid and sugar. If there are warning signs, or your age and history call for it, a look inside the bowel with a colonoscopy may be advised. For difficult cases, specialised tests of the pelvic floor or bowel transit can help, and we will guide you to the right one.

## Myth versus fact

**Myth: You must go every day to be healthy.** Fact: Every few days can be completely normal if it is comfortable and easy.

**Myth: All laxatives damage the bowel.** Fact: Some, used properly under advice, are considered safe for regular use. The problem is using them blindly without finding the cause.

**Myth: Constipation is always a diet problem.** Fact: Medicines, hormones, pelvic floor problems and slow transit are common, and none of them is fixed by diet alone.

## What actually helps

When the cause is clear, treatment usually makes sense quickly. A few steps help almost everyone: build fibre up gradually rather than all at once, drink steadily through the day, and use the natural urge that often comes 20 to 30 minutes after breakfast. A small footstool under your feet on a Western toilet puts you in a squatting position, which straightens the exit and makes passing stool easier.

Beyond that, it depends. A medicine may be switched. A thyroid problem gets treated. Pelvic floor retraining helps the exit problem. The right type of laxative, at the right dose, can be used while the underlying issue is sorted out.
