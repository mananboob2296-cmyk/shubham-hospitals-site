Around the third or fourth month after a baby, a certain kind of question starts to show up in my clinic. It is rarely the first thing a mother says. She talks about the baby's feeding, her sleep, her stitches. Then, as she gets up to leave, she turns back: "One more thing. Down there, things don't feel the way they did."

If that is you, please know that you are in very large company. This is one of the most common and least discussed parts of recovering from childbirth.

## What actually changes

During a vaginal birth the vagina, the perineum and the pelvic floor muscles stretch enormously. Some muscle fibres and nerves are strained, and some women have a tear or a cut that is stitched. Afterwards, tissues need time to heal and regain tone. Breastfeeding keeps oestrogen low, which makes the tissue thinner and drier for as long as you feed.

Women use different words for the result. Looser. Less sensation. A feeling of air or a dragging heaviness. Leaking a little urine with a cough. Pain or dryness during intimacy. A scar that pulls. Feeling that your body no longer feels like yours. All of these are real, and none of them is something to be embarrassed about.

## A quick self-check

Notice whether any of these apply, a few months on:

- A feeling of looseness or reduced grip during intimacy
- A dragging or heavy feeling, especially by evening
- Leaking urine when you cough, sneeze, laugh or run
- Passing wind from the vagina, particularly on bending or exercise
- Difficulty controlling gas or loose stool
- Pain at the scar or with penetration
- A bulge you can feel at the vaginal opening

Some of these, such as a bulge, heaviness or bowel leakage, are worth mentioning sooner. If a bulge is what you notice, our post on [early pelvic organ prolapse](/that-something-coming-down-feeling-early-pelvic-organ-prolapse/) explains it in detail, and the leaking is covered in [stress urinary incontinence](/leaking-when-you-cough-laugh-or-sneeze-stress-urinary-incontinence/).

> **"My delivery was seven months ago. Have I missed the window to fix this?"**
>
> No. Pelvic floor muscles respond to training months and even years after a delivery. Earlier is easier, but there is no cut-off after which exercises stop helping. Many women see the biggest change over the first six to twelve months of consistent work.

## Pelvic floor rehab: the first and best step

This is the part of treatment with the strongest evidence, and it costs nothing but patience.

**Learn the right muscle.** Imagine holding back wind and urine together, and lifting inwards and upwards. Your thighs, buttocks and stomach should stay relaxed, and you should keep breathing. A common mistake is squeezing the buttocks or holding the breath instead.

**Squeeze and let go.** Hold for a few seconds, then release fully for the same count. Letting go completely matters as much as the squeeze. Build up slowly and repeat several times a day.

**Get checked, not just told.** A pelvic floor physiotherapist can examine you gently, confirm you are using the correct muscles, and give a plan matched to your strength. Many women find they were doing it wrongly, which is no failing at all, because nobody teaches this.

**Give it time.** Think in months, not weeks. A mother who does her exercises for a few minutes daily for three to six months usually notices a difference in control, and often in confidence.

**Take care of the rest of you.** Avoid straining with constipation, lift your baby with a breath out, and ease back into exercise rather than jumping into high-impact workouts.

## Myth versus fact

**Myth:** Only women who had big babies or tears feel this way.

**Fact:** It happens after normal deliveries of all kinds, and also after caesareans, because pregnancy itself loads the pelvic floor.

**Myth:** A caesarean means no pelvic floor problem.

**Fact:** It lowers the risk but does not remove it. Pregnancy and hormones play a part too.

**Myth:** If it is still there at one year, it is permanent.

**Fact:** Many women still improve with proper rehabilitation, and other options exist if they do not.

## What if exercises are not enough?

Some women do their work faithfully and are still troubled. Here is how I think about the next steps.

**Look for the cause first.** Dryness from low oestrogen, a scar that is tender, an underlying prolapse or a weak sphincter each need a different answer. A proper examination matters before any treatment is chosen.

**Local treatment.** For dryness and discomfort, lubricants and sometimes low-dose vaginal oestrogen, prescribed after a short check, can help, including during breastfeeding in some women. The same principle is explained in our post on [vaginal dryness and painful intimacy](/vaginal-dryness-and-painful-intimacy-after-menopause-what-actually-helps/).

**Supportive devices.** For prolapse symptoms or leaking, a pessary or a continence device can offer relief while you decide.

**Procedures.** Here I want to be careful and honest. Laser and radiofrequency "tightening" are advertised widely, but the evidence is still limited and regulators have cautioned against overstated claims. Neither should replace rehabilitation, and no one should promise you a result. Where there is a genuine functional problem, such as a significant prolapse, a damaged perineum or a sphincter injury, surgical repair is well established and is chosen for function and comfort. Whatever is on the table, you should hear the benefits, the limits and the alternatives, and you should never feel rushed.

> **"Will my husband notice? Will it ever feel the same?"**
>
> I hear this a lot, and I will not offer a promise. What I can say is that most couples adapt, that strength and sensation usually improve with time and exercise, and that open conversation, lubrication and patience help more than people expect. If pain or loss of sensation persists, that is a medical question we can look into together.
