There is a particular phone call I get more than any other in early pregnancy. It usually comes late at night, the voice is very quiet, and the sentence is almost always the same: "Madam, there is some blood. Is the baby gone?"

I want to answer that question here, honestly, before anyone has to make that call at midnight.

## Bleeding does not automatically mean miscarriage

Light bleeding and spotting in the first three months is common. Many women who bleed early go on to have completely normal pregnancies and healthy babies. So the first thing I say on that phone call is: this is not automatically bad news.

The second thing I say is: we still need to see you.

Both of those are true at the same time, and holding them together is the hard part. Bleeding is a symptom, not a diagnosis. It can come from something harmless or from something that needs treatment today, and the two can look identical from your bathroom floor. You cannot tell them apart by the colour, and neither can I over the phone.

## Where the bleeding can come from

In the first trimester, the usual explanations are these.

**Implantation bleeding** — a small, brief spot of blood around the time the embryo settles into the lining, often mistaken for a very light period.

**The cervix itself** — pregnancy makes the cervix softer and richer in blood vessels, so it can bleed a little after intercourse, after an internal examination, or for no clear reason. A cervical polyp or an infection can do the same.

**A subchorionic collection** — a small pocket of blood that gathers next to the pregnancy sac and leaks out over a few days. Many of these settle quietly on their own.

**Threatened miscarriage** — bleeding while the cervix is still closed and the pregnancy is still growing. A large share of these pregnancies continue normally.

**Miscarriage** — heavier bleeding, usually with cramping, where the pregnancy is not continuing. This one is not caused by lifting a bucket of water, travelling on a bad road, or eating papaya. It is almost always a chromosomal problem in that particular pregnancy, and it is not anyone's fault.

**Ectopic pregnancy** — a pregnancy growing outside the uterus, most often in the tube. This is the one we are always quietly ruling out, because it is the one that can become an emergency.

> **"So how do I know which one is happening to me?"**
>
> You don't, and that is exactly why the answer to spotting is a visit rather than a search on your phone. I have seen textbook-light spotting turn out to be an ectopic, and frightening heavy bleeding turn out to be a subchorionic collection that healed itself. The examination and the scan sort this out in under an hour. Please don't spend a week deciding.

## Signs that mean come now, not tomorrow

Call the hospital immediately — or come straight in — if you have any of these:

- Pain low down on one side, especially if it is sharp or getting worse
- Pain in the tip of your shoulder
- Feeling faint, dizzy, cold or clammy
- Heavy bleeding that soaks a pad in an hour, or large clots
- Fever, or discharge with a bad smell
- Bleeding after any injury or fall

The first three on that list are the ones people ignore, and they are the ones that matter most. Shoulder pain with early pregnancy bleeding sounds unrelated. It isn't.

> **"I passed a small clot. Should I bring it with me?"**
>
> If it came away easily and you have kept it, yes, bring it — it sometimes helps us. But please do not go looking for tissue or delay coming in to collect something. Your safety comes first, and we can work it out without it.

## What actually happens when you come in

Knowing the sequence takes some of the fear out of it.

We start by listening — how many weeks you are, when the bleeding started, how heavy, whether there is pain, your previous pregnancies. Then a general check of your pulse and blood pressure, and a gentle examination to see where the blood is coming from and whether the cervix is open or closed.

Next is usually a scan, generally a transvaginal one in early pregnancy because it sees far more at this stage. It is safe and it does not cause miscarriage. We are looking for the sac in the right place, the size, and a heartbeat if you are far enough along. If it is too early to be sure, we add a blood test and repeat it in a couple of days, and I would rather do that than give you a confident answer I cannot stand behind. Our guide to [pregnancy scans and when each one is done](/blogs/pregnancy-scans-explained-which-scan-when-why-in-amravati/) explains how the rest of the scan schedule works.

We will also check your blood group. If you are Rh negative and you are bleeding, you may need an anti-D injection, and the timing of it matters — this alone is a good reason not to sit at home.

## Three things I wish people would stop believing

**"Bed rest will save it."** Weeks of lying flat has not been shown to prevent miscarriage, and staying immobile for long stretches brings its own problems. Sensible rest, no heavy lifting, no intercourse for a while — that is usually the honest advice.

**"Something I did caused this."** Not travelling, not climbing stairs, not the food you ate, not one argument. Early miscarriage is overwhelmingly about the chromosomes of that pregnancy. I have watched too many women carry blame that was never theirs.

**"I'll mention it at my next appointment."** Please don't. In early pregnancy, bleeding is one of the few things where the same day genuinely matters.
