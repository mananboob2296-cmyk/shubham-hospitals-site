A woman in her late thirties sat across from me recently and said, "Doctor, I've had bad periods for years. Everyone told me it's normal after children." Her scan had been reported as "bulky uterus", and nothing more had been said. That one phrase hides a lot of women, and many of them have adenomyosis.

## What adenomyosis actually is

Every month the lining of the womb (the endometrium) builds up and sheds. In adenomyosis, some of that lining tissue has settled *inside* the muscular wall of the uterus. It still responds to your hormones, so with each cycle it swells and bleeds inside the muscle, which has nowhere to let it out. The wall thickens, the uterus becomes enlarged and tender, and periods become heavy and painful.

It is most often diagnosed in women in their thirties and forties, though it can be found earlier. It is not cancer, and it is not caused by anything you did.

## Why it gets missed

There are a few reasons. The symptoms look like a dozen other things, from fibroids to hormonal imbalance. A routine scan may only say "uterus bulky" or "mildly enlarged". And heavy, painful periods are so widely treated as normal that many women never mention them until they are anaemic or exhausted.

## Adenomyosis vs fibroids vs endometriosis

This is the confusion I untangle most often in clinic, so here is the plain version.

- **Fibroids** are distinct, rounded lumps of muscle tissue, a bit like a ball sitting in or on the wall. They are well-defined and can be counted and measured. (We cover them in our [fibroids post](/uterine-fibroids-do-they-always-need-surgery/).)
- **Adenomyosis** has no ball. The whole wall, or a part of it, is thickened and spongy because lining tissue is scattered through the muscle. The edges are blurred.
- **Endometriosis** is lining-like tissue *outside* the uterus: on the ovaries, tubes or pelvic surfaces. (See our [endometriosis guide](/understanding-endometriosis-symptoms-diagnosis-and-treatment-in-amravati/).)

Here's the catch: they can coexist. A woman can have adenomyosis and fibroids, or adenomyosis and endometriosis, which is partly why symptoms are so hard to pin on one cause. If you want the broader picture of period pain, our [painful periods post](/why-are-my-periods-so-painful-a-gynecologist-explains/) covers the common causes, so I won't repeat them here.

> **"Doctor, how do I know if it's fibroids or adenomyosis? Can I tell from my symptoms?"**
>
> Not reliably. Both cause heavy periods. Clues pointing towards adenomyosis are pain that builds up and lasts through the period, a uterus that feels tender when pressed, and heavy bleeding without an obvious lump on the scan. But the scan and examination decide, not the symptoms alone.

## A quick self-check

Worth mentioning to a doctor if you notice:

- Periods that are heavy enough to need changing a pad every one to two hours
- Cramps that start before your period and last well into it
- A heavy or dragging feeling low in the abdomen
- Pain during intimacy, particularly deep inside
- Tiredness, breathlessness or dizziness that could point to low haemoglobin
- Periods that have steadily worsened over the years

## What to expect at your appointment

I usually begin by listening: the pattern of your bleeding, how pain affects your week, whether you want children. Then a gentle pelvic examination and a transvaginal ultrasound. On the scan we look for a rounded uterus, one wall thicker than the other, small cysts in the muscle, and a blurred boundary between lining and muscle. A blood count checks for anaemia. If the picture is unclear, or if surgery is being planned, an MRI can help. None of this is painful beyond mild discomfort.

> **"If it's not fibroids, is it dangerous?"**
>
> Adenomyosis is benign. It can seriously affect your quality of life and cause anaemia if ignored, but it is not a cancer and does not turn into one.

## Treatment: it depends on what you want

There is no single answer. We decide together, based on your age, symptoms, and whether you plan a pregnancy.

- **Medicines** such as anti-inflammatory painkillers and tranexamic acid for heavy flow.
- **Hormonal treatment**, including a hormone-releasing IUD or progestin tablets, which often settle bleeding and pain.
- **Iron and correcting anaemia**, which matters as much as the pain treatment.
- **Surgery** in selected women, either removing the affected tissue while preserving the uterus, or other uterus-sparing options, when fertility is a priority and medicines are not enough.
- **Hysterectomy**, only when symptoms remain severe and the family is complete. Where it is needed, it can often be done by keyhole surgery; see our [hysterectomy guide](/hysterectomy-types-recovery-and-the-laparoscopic-option/) and our [advanced laparoscopy and hysteroscopy page](/treatments/advanced-laparoscopy-hysteroscopy/).

> **"Will it go away on its own?"**
>
> Often it eases after menopause, when hormones fall, but that can be many years of suffering away. You do not have to wait for that.
