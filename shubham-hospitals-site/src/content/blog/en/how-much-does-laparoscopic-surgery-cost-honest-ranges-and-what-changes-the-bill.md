In three decades of surgical practice I've noticed something odd. Patients will ask me detailed questions about anaesthesia, stitches and recovery — and then hesitate at the one question sitting heaviest on their mind: *"Doctor, what will all this cost?"*

So let me answer it the way I do across the desk. Openly, with real numbers, and with an honest explanation of why the same operation can carry two very different price tags.

## Why there is never one fixed price

Keyhole surgery uses specialised equipment — a camera, insufflation, fine instruments, and often consumables like staplers, clips or mesh that are used once and discarded. The bill is a package of the surgeon's fee, anaesthesia, theatre charges, those consumables, your room category and how long you stay. Change any one of those and the number moves.

That isn't hospitals being cagey. It is genuinely how the arithmetic works. What you are entitled to is a hospital that can show you where the money goes.

> **"Why does the same gallbladder operation cost ₹45,000 in one hospital and ₹1.5 lakh in another?"**

A fair question, and the answer is rarely that someone is cheating you. Room category alone can change a bill dramatically — a general ward and a deluxe room are different products. Then there is the seniority of the surgical and anaesthesia team, the grade of consumables (a hernia mesh ranges from basic to premium lightweight varieties), and the city itself, because rent and staffing costs flow into every bill. A metro corporate hospital carries overheads that a district hospital does not.

## What genuinely moves your bill

- **Complexity of your case.** An acutely inflamed gallbladder or a large, stuck hernia takes longer in theatre than a straightforward one, and theatre time costs money.
- **Emergency versus planned surgery.** A burst appendix at two in the morning involves more than the same appendix removed on a scheduled morning list.
- **Other health conditions.** Diabetes, heart disease or obesity may mean extra tests, closer monitoring, or a day longer in hospital.
- **Consumables you choose.** Where options genuinely exist, your surgeon should explain what the costlier one buys you — and say plainly when the basic one is perfectly good.
- **Length of stay.** Most keyhole patients go home in one to two days. That is a real part of why the total works out lower than open surgery.

> **"Will my insurance cover this?"**

In most cases yes — these are standard, medically accepted procedures covered by the large majority of health policies. Check three things before admission: whether we are on your insurer's cashless network, whether your policy has a waiting period for that condition, and whether there is a cap on room rent. That last one catches people out, because choosing a higher room category can shrink what the insurer pays for everything else. Our billing desk will help you get pre-authorisation sorted before surgery rather than after.

## Questions worth asking before you say yes

When you are handed an estimate, run through this list. Any hospital doing honest work will answer all five without flinching.

- Is this a fixed package or an estimate that can grow, and what exactly does it include?
- What is *not* included — implants, pre-operative tests, medicines after discharge?
- If a complication extends my stay, how is that charged?
- Which mesh or consumable is being used, and were there options?
- Who is actually performing the surgery?

## Myth versus fact

**Myth:** "Keyhole surgery is a luxury — open surgery is the economical choice."

**Fact:** The theatre charges can be somewhat higher, but the shorter stay, fewer wound complications and much earlier return to work usually make the total cost of the episode comparable, and often lower once lost wages are counted. Surgery that keeps you in bed for a month is not cheap.

## Talk numbers before you talk dates

You should never feel awkward asking a surgeon about cost. It is part of informed consent, as much as the risks and the benefits are — and in my experience the families who ask early are the ones who cope best afterwards.
